# Take·5 — recording scripts, draft 1 (2026-07-24)

*Five day-intros, two new guided practices, the shared ritual lines, and the closes.
Written for Jonny's register: warm, plain, short sentences. No exclamation points on
instructions. "Settled," never "anxiety" (the word appears nowhere in these scripts).
No medical promises: the science is described, outcomes are invited, never guaranteed.*

**Recording notes (same discipline as the July 14 session):**
- voice-recorder workflow, quiet room, close mic, a touch louder than feels natural.
- One idea per breath. Let the periods be real pauses.
- The day-intros can run loose and human. The practice cues must leave ROOM —
  the silence between cues is part of the product (the Take10 rule: guidance thins).
- Deliberate smile on the closes. The listener can hear it.

---

## Shared lines (record once, reused every day)

**S1 · The ritual opener (every day starts with these exact words):**
> "Take five. Sit tall, let your hands rest, and take one slow breath."

**S2 · The return (every day ends near these words):**
> "Before you go. Notice one thing that feels different than it did five minutes ago. That's yours now."

**S3 · Course welcome (played once, before Day 1, or lives in email one):**
> "Welcome to Take Five. Five days. A few minutes a day. Each day I'll show you one small thing your body can do for your mind. Nothing to learn, nothing to get right. Just five little experiments, and you're the scientist."

## Day 1 — The second heart *(soleus raises · practice assets exist as calfpump)*

**D1-intro (~70s):**
> "Day one. I want to introduce you to a muscle you've been sitting on your whole life.
> It's called the soleus. It lives low in your calf, and it has a nickname: the second heart. Every time your heel rises, it squeezes, and it pumps blood back up toward the first one.
> Here's the part I love. A few years ago, researchers found that when this one small muscle works while you sit, it keeps working on your blood sugar for hours. Not the gym. Not a run. Heels rising, toes planted, in a chair.
> And a steadier body makes it a whole lot easier to have a steadier mind.
> So that's today's experiment. Sixty seconds of slow heel raises. Sit tall for me."

**D1-practice:** existing `calfpump` voice lines carry the minute.

**D1-close (~15s):**
> "That's day one. Your second heart says thank you. Tomorrow: the two muscles that carry your whole day. See you then."

## Day 2 — Let the shoulders fall *(slow shoulder rolls · practice assets exist as rolls)*

**D2-intro (~65s):**
> "Day two. Do a quick check for me, right now. Where are your shoulders?
> If they were somewhere up near your ears, you're in good company. Shoulders are where a busy day likes to live. They brace when we concentrate, when we brace for an email, when we hold the phone. They creep up, and they forget the way down.
> Today we remind them. Slow rolls, and every single one ends the same way: shoulders dropping, away from the ears. The roll is just the journey. The drop is the destination.
> It looks like a normal desk stretch, because it is one. Nobody will know. Here we go."

**D2-practice:** existing `rolls` voice lines carry the minute.

**D2-close (~15s):**
> "Day two, done. Any time today you catch your shoulders climbing, you know the way down now. Tomorrow we go somewhere almost nobody thinks to look."

---

## Day 3 — Unclench *(jaw release + masseter massage · NEW practice, record fully)*

**D3-intro (~70s):**
> "Day three. Touch the corner of your jaw, just in front of your ear. That's the masseter. For its size, it's one of the strongest muscles you own.
> It's also one of the busiest. Concentration, effort, holding something in. The jaw catches all of it, quietly, all day. Most of us are clenching right now and don't know it.
> Today is the quietest practice in the whole course. We're going to rub that muscle warm, and then we're going to let the jaw simply. . . hang. Teeth apart, lips closed. That's the whole trick.
> Fingertips ready. Find that corner again."

**D3-practice cues (record each as its own clip; long gaps are intentional):**
1. "Small, slow circles. Both sides. No hurry."
2. "If you find a tender spot, stay with it. Gentle."
3. "Now let the hands rest. Teeth apart. Lips closed."
4. "Let the tongue rest low. The jaw just hangs."
5. "Stay here. Heavy jaw. Soft face."
6. "One more slow breath, out through the nose."

**D3-close (~15s):**
> "Notice how much you were holding. That release is available any hour you want it. Tomorrow, day four. Bring your hands."

## Day 4 — Your hands *(hand + forearm self-massage · NEW practice, record fully)*

**D4-intro (~70s):**
> "Day four. Look at your hands for a second.
> They've been working since the moment you woke up. Typing, scrolling, carrying, gripping. Of everything you own, they get the least thanks for the most work.
> Here's what's interesting. Touch is information, and it's information your nervous system trusts. In hospitals, a few minutes of simple hand massage measurably settles people before surgery. Not an app. Not a technique. Just one hand taking care of the other.
> So that's the experiment. One hand massages the other, then we trade. Then the forearms, wrist to elbow. Slow is the whole secret."

**D4-practice cues:**
1. "Start with the palm. Slow thumb circles, like you're smoothing something out."
2. "Find the web between the thumb and the first finger. Press, and breathe out."
3. "Now each finger, base to tip. Take your time."
4. "And trade hands. First hand's turn to rest."
5. "Same palm, same circles. Slow."
6. "Now the forearm. Squeeze and slide, wrist to elbow."
7. "Other side. Wrist to elbow."
8. "Let both hands rest. Heavy and warm."

**D4-close (~15s):**
> "Your hands work for you all day. That was you, working for them. Last day tomorrow. It's my favorite."

---

## Day 5 — Look up, breathe out *(slow head turns + physiological sigh · sigh assets exist)*

**D5-intro (~75s):**
> "Day five. Last one, and it ties the whole week together.
> Here's what the last four days had in common. Your body has dials. Where your shoulders sit. What your jaw holds. What your hands are doing. Your mind reads those dials constantly, and it takes their word seriously.
> Today, two more. The first is your neck: slow turns, letting your gaze travel with your breath. The second is the oldest dial there is: the long exhale. It's the body's built-in message that says, we're okay.
> Turn slow, breathe out long, and let's finish this thing."

**D5-practice cues (head turns; then existing sigh lines take over):**
1. "Breathe out slow, and let your head turn to one side. Easy. Only as far as it wants."
2. "Breathe in, and come back to the middle."
3. "Out again, other side. Let the eyes travel too."
4. "In, and back to center."
5. "Two more each way, at your own pace. Long exhales."
6. "Now come back to the middle, and we'll finish with the exhale in its purest form."

*(→ existing physiological-sigh clips run here.)*

**D5-close / course completion (~25s):**
> "And that's Take Five. Five days, five dials, and you turned every one of them.
> Here's the real secret: you don't need me for any of this anymore. But if you'd like me around anyway, the full app is coming, and as a founding member, you'll be first through the door.
> Take care of yourself. You clearly know how."
